# Safety first — read this before every build

Every experiment in this folder is designed to be done safely by a careful beginner, **with an adult
supervising anyone under 18**. Nothing here is worth an injury. If a step in any tutorial ever seems to
conflict with this page, **this page wins**.

> [!CAUTION]
> **Cosmic Codex never teaches you to make propellant, rocket motors, igniters, ejection charges,
> fireworks or anything pyrotechnic.** Do not mix chemicals to make things burn, fizz violently or
> explode, and do not modify commercial motors. Real rocketry is safe *because* the dangerous part is
> made by a licensed manufacturer and certified by an independent body.

---

## 1. Rules that apply to everything

| Rule | Why |
|---|---|
| Wear **eye protection** (ANSI Z87.1 / EN 166 safety glasses) whenever anything is launched, pressurised, cut or hot. | Eyes do not heal like skin. |
| Never point any rocket — paper, water or model — at a person, animal, vehicle, building or aircraft. | A 60 g bottle at 20 m/s carries as much energy as a thrown baseball. |
| Launch **outdoors**, in an open field, away from roads, power lines, trees and airports. | Recovery must never require climbing or entering a road. |
| Use a **countdown** and make sure everyone is watching. | Surprise is what injures people. |
| Do not approach a misfire straight away (§3). | Delayed ignition and stored pressure are real. |
| Never look at the Sun, directly or through any optics (§5). | Permanent retinal damage happens without pain. |
| Know where your first-aid kit and a bucket of water are. | Minor burns and cuts are the common injuries. |

## 2. Model rockets (Level 2 and up): the NAR Model Rocket Safety Code

In the United States, model rocketry is governed by three layers: the **National Association of
Rocketry (NAR) Model Rocket Safety Code**, the **Federal Aviation Administration (FAA)** rules in
**14 CFR Part 101**, and your state/local fire code, which usually adopts **NFPA 1122, *Code for Model
Rocketry*** from the **National Fire Protection Association (NFPA)**.

**Read the official, current text of the code before your first launch:**
<https://www.nar.org/ModelRocketSafetyCode> (NAR; the version current when this page was written is
dated August 2012). The eleven rules, summarised — the official text is authoritative:

1. **Materials** — only lightweight, non-metal parts for the nose, body and fins.
2. **Motors** — only **certified, commercially made** model rocket motors; never tamper with them or use
   them for anything the manufacturer does not recommend.
3. **Ignition system** — an **electrical** launch system and electrical motor igniters, with a **safety
   interlock** in series with the launch switch, and a launch switch that springs back to "off".
4. **Misfires** — if the rocket does not launch, remove the safety interlock or disconnect the battery and
   **wait 60 seconds** after the last attempt before anyone approaches.
5. **Launch safety** — countdown; everyone paying attention and at least **15 ft (4.6 m)** away for
   D motors or smaller, **30 ft (9 m)** for larger ones.
6. **Launcher** — a launch rod, tower or rail within **30° of vertical**, with a **blast deflector**; cap
   the rod or keep its tip above eye level.
7. **Size** — no more than **1,500 g (53 oz)** at lift-off, **125 g (4.4 oz)** of propellant, or
   **320 N·s** of total impulse.
8. **Flight safety** — never at targets, into clouds, or near aircraft; no flammable or explosive payload.
9. **Launch site** — outdoors, in an open area at least as large as the table below, in winds no stronger
   than **20 mph (32 km/h)**, with no dry grass near the pad.
10. **Recovery system** — a streamer or parachute so the rocket returns undamaged; only flame-resistant
    recovery wadding.
11. **Recovery safety** — never recover a rocket from power lines, tall trees or other dangerous places.

**Minimum launch-site dimensions** (smallest side of the field):

| Installed total impulse (N·s) | Equivalent motor | Minimum site dimension |
|---|---|---|
| 0.00 – 1.25 | ¼A, ½A | 50 ft (15 m) |
| 1.26 – 2.50 | A | 100 ft (30 m) |
| 2.51 – 5.00 | B | 200 ft (61 m) |
| 5.01 – 10.00 | C | 400 ft (122 m) |
| 10.01 – 20.00 | D | 500 ft (152 m) |
| 20.01 – 40.00 | E | 1,000 ft (305 m) |
| 40.01 – 80.00 | F | 1,000 ft (305 m) |
| 80.01 – 160.00 | G | 1,000 ft (305 m) |
| 160.01 – 320.00 | two Gs | 1,500 ft (457 m) |

### Motor codes (how to read "C6-5")

| Part | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **C** | Total-impulse class. Each letter doubles the range: A = 1.26–2.50 N·s, B = 2.51–5.00, C = 5.01–10.0, D = 10.01–20.0 … |
| **6** | Average thrust in newtons. |
| **5** | Delay in seconds between burnout and the ejection charge that deploys the parachute. "-0" = booster motor, no ejection: **never** use one in a single-stage rocket. |

Store motors in their packaging, away from heat, in a metal ammunition-style box if you keep more than a
few. Dispose of misfired motors as the manufacturer instructs (soak spent/misfired black-powder motors in
water overnight, then bin them).

### FAA 14 CFR Part 101, Subpart C (amateur rockets)

| Class | Definition (14 CFR 101.22) | What it means for you |
|---|---|---|
| **Class 1 — model rocket** | ≤ 125 g propellant, slow-burning propellant, paper/wood/breakable plastic, no substantial metal parts, ≤ 1,500 g including propellant | Everything in Levels 0–2. No FAA notification needed, but you must not create a hazard to people, property or aircraft (§ 101.23). |
| **Class 2 — high-power rocket** | Any amateur rocket beyond Class 1 with total impulse ≤ 40,960 N·s | Requires air-traffic-control notification (§ 101.27) and usually a waiver; fly **only** at an NAR or Tripoli Rocketry Association launch with a certified flyer. See [`40-high-power-rocketry-certification`](40-high-power-rocketry-certification/). |
| **Class 3 — advanced high-power** | Anything beyond Class 2 | FAA authorisation required. Out of scope for this repository. |

Official text: <https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-F/part-101/subpart-C>.
Outside the US, check your national rules (e.g. UK: CAA and the UK Rocketry Association; Canada:
Transport Canada and the Canadian Association of Rocketry; Australia: CASA and AMRS).

### NFPA 1122

*NFPA 1122, Code for Model Rocketry* is the fire-safety standard most US states and cities adopt by
reference; the NAR code above is built to agree with it. Free read-only access (registration required):
<https://www.nfpa.org/product/nfpa-1122-code/p1122code>. Some cities and parks ban all launches or need a
permit — **ask your local fire marshal** and get the landowner's permission. The easiest way to fly
legally and safely is at a **NAR section launch** (use "Find a club" on <https://www.nar.org/>).

## 3. Misfire procedure (model rockets)

1. Remove the safety key / interlock **and** disconnect the battery.
2. Wait **60 seconds** (NAR code rule 4). Everyone stays behind the safety line.
3. Only the launch officer approaches. Check the igniter from the side, never over the nozzle.
4. Replace the igniter (commercial igniters only) and repeat the countdown from the start.

## 4. Water rockets (Level 1)

Pressurised plastic bottles store a surprising amount of energy. Follow these limits even if you see
higher pressures online.

| Rule | Limit |
|---|---|
| Maximum pressure, this project | **60 psi (414 kPa gauge)**; 30–45 psi is plenty to learn from. NASA Glenn's guidance suggests ≈ 50 psi for classrooms and never above 90 psi. |
| Bottle | **Carbonated-drink** PET bottles only (designed for pressure). Never water/juice/oil bottles. Never glass. |
| Bottle condition | Retire after **10–15 launches**, and immediately if creased, scratched, cloudy, dented, sun-faded, heated or cut. Never use a bottle that has been glued with solvent near the pressure wall. |
| Launcher | A **remote pull-string release** (≥ 5 m string) and pumping through a long hose, so nobody is near the bottle while it is under pressure. Anchor the launcher. |
| People | Eye protection for **everyone**. Nobody stands over or beside a pressurised rocket. Rope off a launch area; observers several metres back. Ear plugs above 45 psi. |
| Field | At least **30 m** across, grass, no people downrange, wind at your back pushes the rocket away from spectators. |
| Aborts | To abort, release the pressure through the pump's bleed valve or unscrew the pump hose from the far end — never approach and pull the bottle off. |
| PVC | PVC pipe is **not rated for compressed air** by its manufacturers because it can shatter. Keep pressurised PVC short, use Schedule 40 or better, stay below 60 psi, never glue-and-use the same day (let solvent cement cure 24 h), and wrap exposed PVC with tape. Replace any cracked or sun-bleached PVC. |
| Water fill | Never more than half full. |

Sources: NASA Glenn Research Center, *Water Rocket Safety*
(<https://www.grc.nasa.gov/www/k-12/rocket/BottleRocket/safety.htm>) and Scouting America's water-rocket
safety moment (<https://www.scouting.org/health-and-safety/safety-moments/water-bottle-rockets/>).

## 5. The Sun (experiment 03 and any daytime observing)

- **Never look at the Sun** directly, through a pinhole, a camera screen for long, binoculars,
  a telescope, a phone camera zoomed on the Sun, or sunglasses. Pinhole projection means you look at the
  **image on the screen with your back to the Sun**.
- Never point a telescope, binoculars or camera lens at the Sun without a certified solar filter
  (**ISO 12312-2**) securely fitted over the **front** of the optics. Eyepiece "sun filters" can crack.
- If you use a mirror projector, never let the reflected beam cross anyone's eyes or a road, and cover
  the mirror when you are not using it.
- Supervise children closely.

## 6. Dry ice and alcohol (experiment 12)

| Hazard | Precaution |
|---|---|
| Dry ice is −78.5 °C | Insulated gloves and tongs; never touch it with bare skin; never put it in your mouth. |
| CO₂ gas | It sublimates into carbon dioxide gas, which pools low. Use a **well-ventilated** room, never a car, a closet or a small unventilated room. |
| Pressure | **Never seal dry ice in an airtight container** (screw-top bottle, sealed cooler): it can burst. |
| Isopropyl alcohol (99 %) | **Highly flammable**; vapours are heavier than air. No flames, sparks or heaters nearby; use an LED torch, not a hot lamp. Cap the bottle; avoid breathing vapour; eye protection when pouring. |
| Disposal | Let leftover dry ice sublimate in a ventilated place out of reach of children and pets. Do not pour it down a drain. |

## 7. Electronics, batteries and 3D printing (Levels 1–2)

- **Lithium-polymer (LiPo) cells**: only use cells with a built-in protection circuit, charge them with a
  proper single-cell charger on a non-flammable surface, never unattended, and retire any puffy cell.
  Consider alkaline or nickel-metal-hydride (NiMH) AAA cells for your first payload.
- **Soldering**: eye protection, a ventilated area or fume extractor, lead-free solder, wash your hands.
- **3D printers**: never leave a print running while nobody is home; keep the printer on a stable,
  non-flammable surface; ventilate when printing ABS/ASA.
- **Printed rocket parts** must stay within NAR rule 1 (no metal nose, body or fins) and the 1,500 g
  limit. Printed parts near a motor get hot: use PETG or ASA there, not PLA.

---

## 8. Emergencies

Cold running water for 20 minutes on any burn; call emergency services (911 in the US) for anything
serious. Report rocketry incidents to the NAR (<https://www.nar.org/>) so the community can learn.
