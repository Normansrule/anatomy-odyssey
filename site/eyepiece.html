Thinking of buying a telescope?

Five things worth knowing first

Aperture matters most

Aperture is the width of the main lens or mirror. It sets how much light you collect (twice the width, four times the light) and how fine the detail can be. Everything else is secondary.

Why so many people start with a Dobsonian

A Dobsonian is a reflector on a simple swivel box. No motors, no wobbly tripod, nothing to set up: point and look. Most of the money goes into the mirror, so it gives the most aperture for the price.

Ignore "600× power!" on the box

Magnification is set by the eyepiece, so any telescope can claim any number. The useful limit is about 2× the aperture in millimetres (140× for 70 mm), and the air usually stops you near 250×. Beyond that you only enlarge the blur.

Binoculars are a great first step

A pair of 10×50s shows the Moon's craters, Jupiter's moons, the Pleiades and Andromeda, needs no setup and never goes out of use. Many observers keep them next to the telescope.

Try real telescopes first, for free

Robotic observatories take pictures for you over the internet, and astronomy clubs hold free star parties. Plan what to look at before you go out.

How this simulator works, and where it cheats

Positions are real. The Moon's phase, tilt and libration come from Jean Meeus's Astronomical Algorithms (the same code as our Moon explorer); planets from NASA Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) orbital elements; Jupiter's four big moons from their mean longitudes (checked against Meeus's worked example to about a quarter of a Jupiter radius). Stars brighter than magnitude 6 are real (Hipparcos catalogue); fainter stars are placed at random with the real average number per square degree.

The optics are real formulas: magnification, field, exit pupil, the Rayleigh and Dawes limits, the limiting-magnitude rule of thumb, light-gathering power and the "2× aperture" limit are the ones on our equations page. Blur adds three things: the Airy disk of the aperture, the air (seeing, split into image motion and blur after Fried) and your eye's 1-arcminute sharpness divided by the magnification. Brightness follows the exit pupil: a telescope can never make a nebula brighter per square arcsecond than your naked eye sees it.

Approximations. The Moon map is procedural (real maria and big craters, invented small ones, about 5 km per pixel), so at high power it runs out of detail long before a real telescope would. Jupiter's belts, the Great Red Spot's longitude, Mars's markings and the sunspots are illustrative. Nebula and galaxy shapes are hand-built models with realistic sizes and surface brightnesses. The eye model (colour fading in dim light, the detection threshold, dark adaptation) is simplified. Telescopes also show the sky tilted at an angle that changes through the night; we only show the flips.
