        Why the sky turns: sidereal and solar days

        Earth spins once relative to the stars in a sidereal day. Because it also moves about 1/365 of the way around the Sun each day, it must turn a little further, about 0.9856°, before the Sun returns to the meridian: the solar day of 24 hours.

        So every star rises about 3 minutes 56 seconds earlier each night. The clock that follows the stars is the Local Sidereal Time (LST): the Right Ascension (RA) now crossing your meridian.         Why your sky depends on latitude

        The celestial pole stands at an altitude equal to your latitude, so Polaris is a compass and a sextant at once. Stars closer to the pole than that never set; stars as close to the opposite pole never rise:

        Why it depends on the season

        The Sun creeps eastward along the ecliptic by about 1° a day, so the part of the sky that is opposite the Sun, and up at midnight, moves by 2 hours of RA each month. Orion rules winter evenings and Scorpius summer ones because the Sun stands in Scorpius in late November and in Orion's neighbour Taurus in early June.
