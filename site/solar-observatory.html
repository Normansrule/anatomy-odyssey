Beyond the disk

An eclipse on demand

The corona is a million times fainter than the solar surface, so we normally see it only during a total eclipse. The Large Angle and Spectrometric Coronagraph (LASCO) on the Solar and Heliospheric Observatory (SOHO) makes its own eclipse with a metal disk, 1.5 million km sunward of Earth, and has done so every few minutes since 1996.

The forecasters' Sun

GOES-19 Solar Ultraviolet Imager (SUVI)

The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) flies its own solar telescope on its Geostationary Operational Environmental Satellite (GOES) weather satellites. SUVI photographs the Sun in six extreme-ultraviolet channels several times a minute, and the Space Weather Prediction Center (SWPC) uses the images to issue flare and storm alerts. These are the latest from NOAA's primary satellite.

The Sun on the day you choose

Before you look up

How to observe the Sun safely

Never look at the Sun directly, and never through binoculars, a telescope, a camera or a phone zoom without a proper solar filter. Retinal burns are painless and permanent.

  • Eclipse glasses must meet the international standard ISO 12312-2 and come from a reputable supplier. Check that they have no scratches or holes, and supervise children. Ordinary sunglasses, even very dark ones, are not safe.
  • Never use eclipse glasses behind binoculars or a telescope: the focused light burns through them in seconds.
  • Telescopes and binoculars need a certified solar filter that covers the front of the instrument (full aperture), fitted securely. Eyepiece "sun filters" can crack; never use one.
  • Projection is the safest of all: a pinhole or a small mirror throws an image of the Sun onto a white card, and you look only at the card, with your back to the Sun.
  • Hydrogen-alpha telescopes, which show prominences like the 304 Å channel above, are dedicated instruments; learn to use one at a local astronomy club.
Your own solar observatory

Experiment 03 · Pinhole Sun and sunspots

For about $0 to $5, a long cardboard tube and a pinhole project a Sun you can draw every day. On active days sunspots appear, and a week of drawings measures how fast the Sun spins, about 25 days at the equator (26 as seen from the moving Earth). Compare your drawings with the "Visible" channel on the dial.

Who is watching

The telescopes behind the pictures

A fleet from four space agencies and a mountaintop in Hawaiʻi watches the Sun around the clock, each from a different place and for a different purpose.

NASA · 2010

Solar Dynamics Observatory (SDO)

Inclined geosynchronous orbit, 36,000 km up, in constant view of its ground station in New Mexico.

Atmospheric Imaging Assembly (AIA): four telescopes, ten wavelengths, 4096 × 4096 pixels every 12 s. Helioseismic and Magnetic Imager (HMI): surface light, motion and magnetic field. Extreme ultraviolet Variability Experiment (EVE).

ESA & NASA · 1995

Solar and Heliospheric Observatory (SOHO)

Halo orbit around the Sun–Earth Lagrange point L1, 1.5 million km sunward.

Twelve instruments, including LASCO's C2 and C3 coronagraphs and the Extreme ultraviolet Imaging Telescope (EIT). Three decades on, still the backbone of Coronal Mass Ejection (CME) forecasting and the most prolific comet hunter in history.

NOAA & NASA · 2024

GOES-19 and SUVI

Geostationary orbit at 75.2° W, operational as GOES-East since April 2025.

A weather satellite that also watches the Sun: SUVI images six extreme-ultraviolet channels, and the Extreme ultraviolet and X-ray Irradiance Sensors (EXIS) measure the X-ray flux that sets a flare's class (C, M, X).

NSF · 2020

Daniel K. Inouye Solar Telescope

Summit of Haleakalā, Maui, about 3,000 m up. Run by the National Solar Observatory (NSO).

A 4-metre mirror, the largest solar telescope in the world, resolving features on the Sun as small as about 30 km. Instruments include the Visible Broadband Imager (VBI), the Visible Spectro-Polarimeter (ViSP), the Visible Tunable Filter (VTF) and two infrared spectro-polarimeters.

ESA & NASA · 2020

Solar Orbiter

Elliptical orbit reaching 42 million km from the Sun (0.28 AU), tilted further out of the ecliptic by each Venus flyby: 17° in 2025, about 33° by 2029.

Ten instruments, among them the Extreme Ultraviolet Imager (EUI), the Polarimetric and Helioseismic Imager (PHI) and the Metis coronagraph, plus sensors that sample the solar wind it flies through.

NASA · 2018

Parker Solar Probe

Seven Venus flybys shrank its orbit until, from 24 Dec 2024, each perihelion passes 6.1 million km from the surface at about 690,000 km/h.

Behind an 11.4 cm carbon-composite heat shield: the Wide-field Imager (WISPR), FIELDS, the Solar Wind Electrons Alphas and Protons (SWEAP) investigation and the Integrated Science Investigation of the Sun (IS☉IS).

ISRO · 2023

Aditya-L1

India's first solar mission, in a halo orbit around L1 since 6 January 2024.

Seven payloads from the Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO) and partners, including the Visible Emission Line Coronagraph (VELC) and the Solar Ultraviolet Imaging Telescope (SUIT), which images the Sun from 200 to 400 nm.

ISRO mission page ↗
JAXA · 2006

Hinode

Sun-synchronous polar orbit about 680 km up, in sunlight for most of the year.

Japan's Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA) observatory, built with NASA and the United Kingdom: a 50 cm Solar Optical Telescope (SOT), an X-Ray Telescope (XRT) and an EUV Imaging Spectrometer (EIS).
