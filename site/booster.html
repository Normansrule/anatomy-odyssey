A Falcon 9-class first stage has just let go of its upper stage. Bring it home: flip, brake, steer with the grid fins and finish with a one-engine hoverslam. It cannot hover, so the engine has to reach zero speed exactly at zero height.

The hoverslam

—

One engine at its minimum throttle still pushes harder than the empty stage weighs (thrust-to-weight ≈ 2), so the booster cannot hover. It must light at the right height so the speed reaches zero just as the legs touch. Light too early and it stops in mid-air and climbs; too late and it hits. The amber burn now marker on the altitude tape is this formula, live, at your current throttle; drag (D) helps too, but fades as the stage slows, so only half of it is counted.

The autopilot plans at 80 % throttle, so it can push harder or softer, and flares for the last 40 m at a gentler deceleration: —.

Why land a booster?

The first stage is the expensive part: about 60 % of the cost of the whole rocket, by Elon Musk's account; in 2020 he put the cost of refurbishing a landed booster at about $250,000 and the marginal cost of a reused launch at about $15 million (claims reported by ElonX, not audited figures). Landing is not free either. The propellant kept for the return is propellant not used to push the payload: the published geostationary transfer orbit (GTO) capacity is 8.3 t when the stage is thrown away, 5.5 t with a drone-ship landing and 3.5 t with a return to the launch site.

That is why far-flying missions land on a ship: turning around to fly home (the boostback) costs much more than letting the stage coast down-range.

The entry burn

Around 55 km the stage is falling at over 1.5 km/s into thickening air. Aerodynamic heating grows roughly with √ρ · v³ (Sutton–Graves) and the load with q = ½ρv², so a few hundred m/s removed up high pays off many times over. Three engines fire into the oncoming flow; their exhaust wraps back around the stage as a hot but protective cushion.

—

Grid fins

Four lattice fins swing out near the top of the stage. The air flows through their small cells, so they still work at large angles of attack and at supersonic speed, and need little force to twist. They were aluminium at first; the current ones are cast and machined titanium, which SpaceX has described as surviving re-entry without the ablative coating the aluminium fins needed. With the engines off, the long body itself acts as a wing: to glide toward the target, the stage tilts its top away from it. Once the landing engine lights, thrust dominates and the steering sense flips.
