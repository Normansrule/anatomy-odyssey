    Orbit regimes

    Low Earth Orbit (LEO) · 160–2,000 km
    90–127 minutes per lap. Space stations, Starlink, Earth imaging. Close enough for fast internet and sharp pictures, low enough that the thin upper atmosphere slowly drags things down.
    Medium Earth Orbit (MEO) · 2,000–35,786 km
    Navigation: GPS at 20,200 km, Galileo at 23,222 km. Each satellite is visible from a large part of the planet for hours.
    Geostationary Orbit (GEO) · 35,786 km
    One lap per sidereal day (23 h 56 min) above the equator, so the satellite hangs still in your sky: weather imagers and television.
    Highly Elliptical Orbit (HEO)
    Molniya orbits (inclination 63.4°, where J2 stops the perigee drifting) loiter for hours over high northern latitudes.
    Sun-synchronous orbit (SSO)
    A near-polar LEO tilted about 98° so Earth's equatorial bulge (J2) turns the orbit plane 0.986° a day, exactly keeping pace with the Sun. The satellite crosses each latitude at the same local time, so shadows are comparable from pass to pass.

    How long is an orbit?

    Kepler's third law gives the period T from the semi-major axis a and Earth's gravitational parameter μ = 398,600.44 km³/s². Circular speed follows from v = √(μ/r).

    Why Starlink “trains” appear

    Up to about 29 Starlink satellites leave a Falcon 9 together and are released at a low parking orbit near 280–300 km. For days they fly in a tight line on nearly the same orbit, so after sunset people see a string of pearls crossing the sky. Each satellite then raises its orbit with a krypton or argon Hall-effect thruster to about 550 km, and because a lower orbit is faster, the ones that start raising first drift apart until the train dissolves into the shell. Their flat solar arrays also reflect sunlight strongly in the early days, before the satellites turn edge-on to reduce glare.

    At orbital speed a 1 cm fragment carries the energy of a hand grenade, so the European Space Agency (ESA) warns that without better disposal some orbits could become unusable. Its Space Environment Report 2025 counts about 40,000 tracked objects (about 11,000 of them working payloads), at least 3,000 new tracked fragments from break-ups in 2024, and intact satellites or rocket bodies re-entering more than three times a day.
