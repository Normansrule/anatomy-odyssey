Three special radii

A non-rotating (Schwarzschild) black hole of mass M is described entirely by one length, the Schwarzschild radius:

The simulation works in “geometric units” where G = c = M = 1, so the horizon sits at r = 2 and distances are measured in M (= GM/c²).

r = 2MEvent horizon

Nothing, not even light, gets back out.

r = 3M = 1.5 rsPhoton sphere

Light can orbit here — unstably. Rays that skim it make the thin photon ring.

r = 6M = 3 rsInnermost Stable Circular Orbit (ISCO)

Gas inside this radius cannot orbit; it plunges. The disk’s inner edge.

The disk’s colours

A thin Shakura–Sunyaev disk radiates like a stack of blackbodies whose temperature peaks just outside the ISCO:

Light reaching the camera is shifted in frequency by a single factor g that combines gravitational redshift and the Doppler effect of gas orbiting at Keplerian speed (Ω = √(M/r³), about half the speed of light at the ISCO):

Here bz is the ray’s impact parameter about the disk axis. Gas coming towards you has g > 1: it looks bluer and, because intensity scales as g⁴ (Liouville’s theorem: Iν/ν³ is conserved), dramatically brighter. That is relativistic beaming, and it is why one side of the ring outshines the other.

“Interstellar” look. The film’s black hole, Gargantua, was rendered with the same kind of physics by Double Negative (James et al., 2015), but the beaming and colour shift were switched off so audiences would not be confused by a lopsided, blue-and-red disk. Flip the toggle to compare.

What the Event Horizon Telescope saw

The Event Horizon Telescope (EHT) links radio dishes from Hawaii to the South Pole into one Earth-sized virtual telescope using Very Long Baseline Interferometry (VLBI). At a wavelength of 1.3 mm it resolves about 20–25 micro-arcseconds (μas) — enough to read a newspaper in New York from Paris.

It is not a photo of the horizon

The dark centre is the black hole’s shadow: the patch of sky where rays traced back from the telescope end on the horizon. Lensing magnifies it to a radius of √27 M ≈ 5.2 M — about 2.6 times the horizon radius. The bright ring is hot, magnetised plasma glowing at radio wavelengths, piled up near the photon ring.

Why one side is brighter

Plasma near the hole moves at a sizeable fraction of light speed. Gas moving towards us is Doppler-beamed: brighter by roughly g³–g⁴. In M87* the southern half of the ring is brighter because the flow there is coming towards Earth. Tilt the camera to near face-on here (as M87* is seen, about 17° from its jet axis) and switch beaming on and off to see the same effect.

Simulation vs. reality. This page draws a thin, optically thick disk seen in visible colours. The EHT sources are dim, puffy, optically thin flows glowing in radio, and both holes spin. The shadow and photon-ring physics are the same.
