        Why one face?

        Earth's gravity stretched the young Moon into a slight bulge and tidal friction slowed its spin until the bulge stayed pointed at us. It is tidally locked: it turns once on its axis in exactly one orbit.

        Libration: we see 59%

        The spin is steady but the orbit is not. The Moon moves faster near perigee (Kepler's second law), so its face rocks east–west by up to ±7.9°; its axis is tilted 6.7° to its orbit, so it nods north–south by up to ±6.7°. Over time we see 59% of the surface. Press play in Seen from Earth and watch Mare Crisium swing toward the limb and back.

        From new Moon back to new Moon takes 29.53 days (the synodic month), two days longer than one orbit because Earth has moved along its own orbit meanwhile.

        Why eclipses are rare

        The Moon's orbit is tilted 5.1° to Earth's. Most new and full Moons pass above or below the Sun–Earth line; an eclipse needs the Moon at a crossing point (a node) at the right moment. That happens in two "eclipse seasons" about 173 days apart.

        The Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon and about 400 times farther away, so they look the same size and a total solar eclipse is just possible. At apogee the Moon looks too small and the eclipse is annular (a ring).

        The Moon is leaving

        Tides raised by the Moon slow Earth's spin, and the angular momentum goes into the Moon's orbit. Lunar Laser Ranging (LLR) times laser pulses bounced off corner-cube retroreflectors left by Apollo 11, 14 and 15 and on the Soviet Lunokhod 1 and 2 rovers: the Moon recedes by 3.8 cm per year, about as fast as fingernails grow.
