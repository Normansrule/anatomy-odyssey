    Planet spread: the smallest slice of the Sun's sky, in heliocentric longitude, that holds all eight planets. A perfect “alignment” would be 0°; the “planet parades” in the news are when this slice is unusually narrow, as seen from Earth.

    How the positions are computed
    1. Take Standish's Table 1 elements for 1800 to 2050: semi-major axis a, eccentricity e, inclination I, mean longitude L, longitude of perihelion ϖ, node Ω, each drifting linearly with time T in Julian centuries from J2000.
    2. Mean anomaly M = L − ϖ; argument of perihelion ω = ϖ − Ω.
    3. Solve Kepler's equation M = E − e sin E for the eccentric anomaly E with Newton's method: E ← E + (M − E + e sin E) / (1 − e cos E).
    4. In the orbit plane: x = a(cos E − e), y = a√(1 − e²) sin E. Rotate by ω, I and Ω into the J2000 ecliptic.
    5. Distance from Earth = |planet − Earth|; one-way light-time = distance ÷ c (299,792.458 km/s).

    Accuracy: arcminutes, good enough to predict every opposition in the test suite to within a day. The Moon uses the Astronomical Almanac's low-precision series; Ceres uses rounded JPL Small-Body Database elements; the spacecraft are approximate.

    Positions: E. M. Standish, JPL Keplerian Elements for Approximate Positions of the Major Planets. Physical data: NASA Planetary Fact Sheet. Surfaces are procedurally generated impressions, not maps.
