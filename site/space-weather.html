Latest alerts, in plain English

    How to read it

    From a storm on the Sun to lights in your sky

    The Sun blows a thin, hot, magnetised gas, the solar wind, past Earth at 300 to 800 kilometres a second. Most of the time Earth's magnetic field deflects it. When the wind is fast, dense, and carries a magnetic field pointing south, energy pours in: that is a geomagnetic storm.

    Speed

    How fast the wind blows

    Quiet wind: 300 to 450 km/s. Fast streams from coronal holes: 600 to 800 km/s. The front of a Coronal Mass Ejection (CME) can exceed 1,000 km/s, and during the May 2024 storm it ran at 750 to 950 km/s.

    Density

    How much gas it carries

    Usually 2 to 10 protons in every cubic centimetre, a better vacuum than any laboratory. A CME's compressed sheath can pack 30 to 50 or more.

    Bt and Bz

    The magnetic field it drags along

    The Interplanetary Magnetic Field (IMF) is a few nanotesla (nT), about a million times weaker than a fridge magnet. Bt is its total strength; Bz is the north–south part. Southward Bz (negative) is what lets energy in.

    Kp

    How shaken Earth's field is

    The planetary K-index (Kp) summarises magnetometers around the world every 3 hours, from 0 (calm) to 9 (extreme), on a quasi-logarithmic scale. Kp 5 is a G1 storm; Kp 9 is G5.

    Why southward Bz matters: magnetic reconnection

    At the nose of the magnetosphere, Earth's own field points north. If the solar wind's field also points north, the two slide past each other and the wind flows around. If it points south, the two fields are anti-parallel: they break and reconnect, splicing Earth's field onto the solar wind's. The wind then drags those opened field lines back into the tail, where they reconnect again and fling particles down towards the poles. That is the engine of the aurora, first proposed by James Dungey in 1961.

    How long does a CME take to reach Earth?

    A Coronal Mass Ejection (CME) is a billion-tonne cloud of magnetised plasma blasted off the Sun. At a constant speed v it covers the 149.6 million kilometres to Earth, one astronomical unit (AU), in . Real CMEs slow down or speed up as they plough through the ambient solar wind, so forecasters use models such as the Wang-Sheeley-Arge (WSA)–Enlil simulation, but the simple estimate is already close: most arrive in one to three days.

    What space weather does down here

    Radio blackouts R scale

    A flare's X-rays reach Earth in 8 minutes and ionise the lower ionosphere on the sunlit side, soaking up high-frequency (HF) radio used by aircraft, ships and radio amateurs. The X5.8 flare of 11 May 2024 caused an R3 (strong) blackout over the Pacific.

    GPS errors G scale

    Storms churn the ionosphere, bending and delaying Global Positioning System (GPS) signals. In May 2024, farmers across the US Midwest had to stop planting when centimetre-accurate tractor guidance drifted.

    Satellite drag G scale

    Heating swells the upper atmosphere, so low satellites plough through denser air. On 3 February 2022, 49 new Starlink satellites were released very low just as a moderate storm hit; drag rose by up to 50%, and SpaceX said up to 40 of them would re-enter.

    Power grids G scale

    A rapidly changing magnetic field drives currents through the ground and into long power lines, overheating transformers. On 13 March 1989 this collapsed the Hydro-Québec grid in 90 seconds, leaving six million people without power for nine hours.

    Radiation S scale

    Protons accelerated to a large fraction of the speed of light arrive within minutes to hours. Astronauts shelter in shielded parts of their spacecraft, polar flights reroute, and satellite electronics can flip bits.

    Aurora Kp

    Electrons funnelled down field lines hit oxygen and nitrogen 100 to 400 km up. Oxygen glows green at 557.7 nanometres lower down and red at 630 nm higher up; nitrogen adds purple and pink edges.

    Two great storms

    1859

    The Carrington Event

    On 1 September 1859, Richard Carrington and Richard Hodgson independently saw a brilliant white-light flare on the Sun: the first flare ever recorded. About 17.6 hours later the CME struck, implying an average speed near 2,400 km/s. Aurora glowed as far south as the Caribbean, bright enough to read by, and telegraph operators took shocks while some lines kept working with their batteries disconnected, powered by the storm alone.

    Estimates of its strength (disturbance storm-time index, Dst) range from about −850 nT (Siscoe and others, 2006) to −1,760 nT (Tsurutani and others, 2003).

    2024

    The May 2024 "Gannon" storm

    Active region 13664 fired a string of X-class flares and several CMEs on 8 and 9 May 2024. The first arrived about 17:00 UTC on 10 May and drove the first G5 (extreme) storm since 2003, with Kp 9, the magnetic field at L1 reaching 73 nT and Bz down to −50 nT. Aurora was photographed from Los Angeles, Mexico and northern India. It is named after the space physicist Jennifer Gannon, who died that month.
