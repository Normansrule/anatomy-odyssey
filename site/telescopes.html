How professionals get telescope time

Nobody just walks up to the Very Large Telescope

Big observatories give out time by competition. Understanding the pipeline explains why the archives above are full of free data.

  1. 1

    Call for proposals

    Once or twice a year each observatory announces how many hours it has and what its instruments can do.

  2. 2

    Write a proposal

    A short science case plus a technical case: exposure times from the observatory's exposure time calculator, and why this telescope.

  3. 3

    Peer review

    A Time Allocation Committee (TAC) of astronomers ranks the proposals. Many, including the Space Telescope Science Institute (STScI), use dual-anonymous review, so reviewers don't know who wrote each one.

  4. 4

    Scheduling

    Top-ranked programmes are scheduled, often in a queue that runs each one when the sky, Moon and weather suit it. A small reserve, Director's Discretionary Time, covers surprises like a new supernova.

  5. 5

    Observe

    Usually nobody from the team is at the telescope: staff or robots take the data, just like the robotic networks above.

  6. 6

    Archive

    After an exclusive-access period, commonly up to a year and sometimes zero, the data become public in archives like MAST and the ESO Science Archive. That is what you download.

Hours available

Large observatories are routinely oversubscribed: several times more hours are requested than exist, so most good proposals are turned down. (Bars illustrative.) Student routes in: summer research programmes, co-writing a proposal with a supervisor, and the education networks above, which run the same queue scheduling at a smaller scale.
