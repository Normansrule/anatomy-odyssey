    Where the mass goes

    The rocket equation

    Each stage's change in velocity (Δv) depends only on how fast its exhaust leaves (specific impulse, Isp, times g0 = 9.81 m/s²) and on the ratio of its full mass m0 to its empty mass mf. Because of the logarithm, doubling the propellant does not double the Δv: that is why rockets drop empty stages.

    How the numbers are made

    Engines, boosters and payloads come from data/builder-parts.json, each with its source; ≈ marks estimates. A tank holds 80 % of its cylinder volume as usable propellant at the mixture's bulk density (kerosene + oxygen 1,022 kg/m³, methane + oxygen 833, hydrogen + oxygen 361). Its structure weighs σ × (1 + 0.6 m / diameter) of that propellant, with σ = 4.0 % for kerosene, 4.5 % for methane and 6.5 % for hydrogen, checked against the Saturn V and Falcon 9 stages. Interstages weigh 70 kg per m².

    Between sea level and vacuum, thrust falls linearly with the outside pressure (F = Fvac − p·Aexit). The first stage's effective Isp is measured along its simulated flight; until that is ready it is taken 80 % of the way from sea level to vacuum.

    The flight check is a 2-D ascent integrated with fourth-order Runge–Kutta: U.S. Standard Atmosphere 1976, Mach-dependent drag, inverse-square gravity and a rotating Earth (a free 408 m/s launching east from 28.5° N). A flight computer tries about a dozen pitch programs (vertical rise, pitch-over, gravity turn, then closed-loop steering) and keeps the best, so a design fails only if the physics says so. Δv targets beyond orbit are from the Wikipedia "Delta-v budget" article.
