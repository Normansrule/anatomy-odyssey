    How it works

    The three phases of the descent

    The descent was planned in three phases (F. V. Bennett, NASA Technical Note (TN) D-6846), each a separate program in the Lunar Guidance Computer (LGC). The clock shows Ground Elapsed Time (GET) since launch: Powered Descent Initiation (PDI) came at 102:33:05.

    ProgramPhaseWhat happens
    P63BrakingFull thrust, engine pointing almost straight ahead, windows first down then up. Cancels ≈1.5 km/s in 8½ minutes. Ends at High Gate: ≈7,000 ft, ≈4.5 nautical miles out.
    P64ApproachThe LM pitches toward upright so the crew can see the site ahead, 16° below them. The Landing Point Designator (LPD) shows where the computer is going. Ends at Low Gate: 500 ft, 60 ft/s forward, 16 ft/s down.
    P66LandingRate Of Descent (ROD) mode: the commander flies attitude with the hand controller; the computer holds the descent rate he sets, 1 ft/s per click of a switch.
    The guidance equation

    P63 and P64 command an acceleration that is a quadratic function of time (A. R. Klumpp, MIT Draper Laboratory, 1971):

    a = aT − 6 (v + vT) / T + 12 (rT − r) / T²

    r, v are where Eagle is and how fast it moves; rT, vT, aT are the target (High Gate, then the landing point); T is the time to go, chosen so the jerk at the target matches a planned value. Subtract gravity and you get the thrust vector: its direction is the attitude, its size the throttle. The computer re-solved this every 2 seconds. This page uses the same law with its own tuned targets.

    The throttle band. The DPS could throttle between 10 % and 60 % of its 10,500 lbf (46.7 kN) rating, or run at a Fixed Throttle Point (FTP) of 92.5 %. The range between was avoided because of throat erosion and mixture-ratio control (NASA TN D-7143), so the braking phase runs at FTP and the guidance is aimed so that "throttle down" happens about 6½ minutes in — on Apollo 11 at 386 s, two seconds after the prediction.

    Why land with the Sun only 10° high?

    Craters and boulders are only visible by their shadows. With the Sun low behind them (planned 10.9°, at most 13.6°), every bump throws a long shadow toward the crew, and their 16° line of sight to the site stays above the "Sun line", where features would wash out (Bennett, TN D-6846). That tied each landing site to about one day a month, soon after local sunrise, when the Sun stood between 7° and 20° behind the approaching LM.

    Look for the bright halo around Eagle's own shadow: the opposition surge. Looking directly away from the Sun, each grain hides its own shadow. Aldrin saw it: "I got the shadow out there."

    Program alarms 1202 and 1201

    Five minutes after ignition the Display and Keyboard (DSKY) lit the PROG lamp: "It's a 1202." It meant executive overflow: the computer's scheduler had run out of core sets (1201: out of Vector Accumulator, VAC, areas). A mis-phased power supply for the rendezvous radar made its interface steal about 13 % of the computer's time.

    The Executive was designed for exactly this: it restarted, dropped low-priority jobs and kept flying. Guidance Officer Steve Bales, advised by Jack Garman who had written the list of alarm codes, called "Go" within seconds. Turn the alarms on in Settings to see them happen at the same moments as on 20 July.

    The Apollo Guidance Computer (AGC)
    • 2,048 words of erasable magnetic-core memory and 36,864 words of fixed "core rope" memory, woven by hand; 16-bit words.
    • A 2.048 MHz clock, integrated circuits (about 5,600 three-input NOR gates), about 32 kg.
    • The crew talked to it in verbs and nouns: V16 N68 means "monitor (verb 16) the landing data (noun 68)".
    • Its priority-scheduled Executive and restart protection are why the 1202 alarms did not end the landing.
    Contact light, touchdown and propellant

    Probes 5.6 ft (1.71 m) long hung under three footpads — not the ladder leg. When one touched, a blue CONTACT light came on and the crew shut the engine down. The landing gear was certified for up to 10 ft/s (3 m/s) down, 4 ft/s (1.2 m/s) sideways, 6° of tilt and 12° slopes (NASA TN D-6850). Eagle touched down at about 1.7 ft/s down and 2.1 ft/s sideways, 0.8° pitch and 2.6° roll.

    The low-level light latched about 36 s early because the propellant sloshed (TN D-7143), so the "60 seconds" and "30 seconds" calls — seconds to Bingo, when the crew had 20 s to land or abort — sounded worse than reality. About 770 lb were left, 670 lb usable: roughly 45 s of flying on top of the abort reserve (Apollo Lunar Surface Journal), 63.5 s of hover to the emptiest tank (TN D-7143).
