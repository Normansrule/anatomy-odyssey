    Uplink power is the transmitter's output in kilowatts (kW). Downlink power is what reaches the dish, in decibels relative to one milliwatt (dBm): −150 dBm is 10−18 W. Range is the distance to the spacecraft; round-trip light time (RTLT) is how long a signal takes to get there and back. Data rates are in bits per second.

    Why three sites, 120° apart

    Who can see which part of the sky

    As Earth turns, a distant spacecraft rises and sets like a star. Put three big dishes roughly a third of the way around the world from each other and at least one always has it above the horizon.

    Why only 160 bits per second?

    Voyager 1's radio puts out about 23 watts, roughly a fridge light bulb, through a 3.66 m dish. By the time the beam reaches Earth it has spread over a patch far wider than the planet, and a 70 m dish catches less than a billionth of a billionth of a watt.     A dish of diameter D concentrates power by its gain, and the achievable data rate is the received power divided by the energy each bit needs above the receiver's thermal noise:

    Voyager 1's link budget, live

    This budget supports about—
    160

    Model values from the equation atlas (after Ludwig and Taylor, 2002): 23 W at 8.42 GHz (X-band), aperture efficiency η = 0.6, system noise temperature Tsys = 25 K, and Eb/N0 = 7 dB for the error-correcting code plus safety margin. Arraying adds the dishes' collecting areas. Real budgets also count pointing, weather and elevation losses: treat this as order of magnitude.

    So 160 bit/s is not a choice of convenience: it is close to the most a 70 m dish can pull reliably out of the noise from 25 billion km, with a margin for rain and low elevations. Every doubling of distance cuts the possible rate by four. Commands go the other way at just 16 bit/s, but from a transmitter of up to 20 kW, about a million times Voyager's power.
