A restricted N-body simulation

A full simulation would let every star pull on every other star: N² forces. In 1972 Alar and Juri Toomre showed that most of what you see in colliding galaxies needs far less. Put all the mass in the two galaxy centres, move those, and treat the stars as massless test particles that only feel the centres. Tails and bridges appear anyway.

Here each galaxy is a rigid potential made of three spheres: a Hernquist bulge, a Plummer sphere carrying the disk’s mass, and a Hernquist dark-matter halo that holds about 94% of the mass:

Every star feels both galaxies, a = −∇ΦMW(x − XMW) − ∇ΦM31(x − XM31).

Leapfrog on the graphics card

Each star’s position and velocity are stored as pixels of two floating-point textures. One draw call updates all of them at once with the leapfrog (kick–drift) integrator:

Leapfrog is symplectic: it preserves the geometry of phase space, so an isolated disk keeps its size and energy for billions of years instead of slowly spiralling in or out, as simpler methods do. In the Milky Way run the step is Δt = 0.15 time units ≈ 0.71 million years (the smaller presets use 0.47–0.57 million years): about 20 steps per orbit even deep in the bulge, and 14 000 steps for the whole 10-billion-year run. The galaxy centres use the same leapfrog on the CPU, so every star step sees the centres exactly where they are.

Dynamical friction: why they merge

Test particles can’t slow anything down, so the centres get the friction force Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar derived in 1943. A heavy body ploughing through a sea of stars and dark matter pulls a wake behind it, and the wake pulls back:

Orbital energy drains away with every pass until the centres sink together. This is the one calibrated knob in the model: the strength is set to 0.2 × the formula, with ln Λ = 3, so that the 2012 orbit merges about 2 billion years after the first pass, as van der Marel et al. found with full N-body models. Tidal tails and bridges

Gravity weakens with distance, so the side of a galaxy nearest its partner is pulled harder than the centre, and the centre harder than the far side. The disk is stretched along the line between them. Stars on the near side are drawn into a bridge; stars on the far side are left behind in a tail. When the disk spins the same way as the orbit (prograde), those stars stay in step with the passing tidal force for a long time and are flung out in long, thin tails. Retrograde disks barely react — flip a spin switch and compare.

The Toomre result

With just this restricted model, Toomre & Toomre (1972) reproduced the Antennae, the Mice, M51 and Arp 295, and argued that such galaxies are collisions caught in the act rather than exotic objects. In 1977 Alar Toomre went further: merging spirals should leave elliptical galaxies behind. Decades of full N-body simulations (e.g. Barnes & Hernquist 1992) have borne both out.

What this model leaves out

Stars here don’t pull on each other, so disks can’t form real bars or self-gravitating spiral arms, and tails can’t collapse into new dwarf galaxies. The spiral arms you see are a density-wave pattern painted onto young stars (Lin & Shu 1964); they fade once the first pass wrecks the disk. There is no gas, so no starbursts or new stars. The halos are rigid and their stripping is folded into the friction strength. For the real thing, see full simulations such as Cox & Loeb (2008).

The Sun has its own clock. In roughly 5 billion years it runs out of hydrogen in its core and swells into a red giant, so no one on Earth will be watching the merger — and Earth will have been too hot for oceans long before that.

How likely is our merger?

In 2012, Hubble measurements of Andromeda’s sideways motion made a collision look nearly certain: first pass in 3.9 billion years, merger in 5.9 (van der Marel et al. 2012). Gaia Data Release 2 (DR2) in 2019 raised the sideways speed and the miss distance. In 2025, Sawala et al. ran 100 000 Monte Carlo simulations that also include M33 and the Large Magellanic Cloud (LMC). They found only about a 50% chance of a merger within 10 billion years, and about 2% for a collision within 4–5 billion years.
